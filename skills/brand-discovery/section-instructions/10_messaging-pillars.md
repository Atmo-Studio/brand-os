# Messaging Pillars: BRND-10
*Voice & Messaging, dimension 3 of 4*

**Fills:** 3–5 pillar blocks (headline, one-liner, proof points, channel expressions)
**Frameworks:** Message Architecture (Margot Bloomstein, 2012) → `frameworks.md §22`; Positioning-to-Messaging Bridge (Dunford, 2019) → `frameworks.md §23`; Proof Hierarchy (Cialdini, 2006) → `frameworks.md §24`

---

## Failure Modes
- Pillars that are claims without proof: "We deliver world-class results" is a claim. Without specific, provable facts behind it, it's noise. Every pillar needs at least two proof points that a skeptic would accept.
- Too many pillars: more than five and the messaging becomes a list of everything the brand does. Pillars are the *core* claims repeated everywhere, not a comprehensive capability inventory. Three is ideal; five is the ceiling.
- Pillars that don't trace back to positioning: each pillar should be an expression of the positioning statement (BRND-5). If a pillar can't be traced to a specific element of the positioning, it's either off-strategy or the positioning needs updating.
- Channel expressions that are the same claim at different lengths: "We make design accessible" said short, medium, and long is not adaptation. Each channel expression should reflect how the *register* and *entry point* change by context: a homepage leads with the outcome, a cold email leads with the problem, a social post leads with the hook.
- Pillars that any competitor could claim: the substitution test applies here too. If you replace the brand name with a competitor's and the pillar still holds, it's not differentiated.

## Upstream Dependencies

This dimension is the messaging layer on top of BRND-5 (Brand Positioning) and informed by BRND-3 (ICPs), BRND-8 (Brand Story), and BRND-9 (Voice & Tone).

- From BRND-5: the positioning statement is the source. Each pillar should express a facet of the positioning: the unique value, the market category, or the audience truth.
- From BRND-3: the ICP's jobs (functional, emotional, social) help determine which claims matter most to the audience.
- From BRND-8: the brand story structure (especially the guide's authority and the plan) provides raw material for proof points.
- From BRND-9: the voice and tone modes should be applied to channel expressions: the pillar said in a homepage voice vs. a cold email voice.

*If BRND-5 is incomplete, stop: "Messaging pillars are the bridge between your positioning and your actual copy. Without a positioning statement to anchor them, the pillars will drift. Let's finish positioning first." BRND-3, BRND-8, and BRND-9 are helpful but not blocking.*

## Interview Rounds

**Round 1: Identifying the Core Claims**
*Extract: The 3–5 things this brand says over and over — the claims that show up across every channel, whether or not they've been formalized.*

- If you could only tell a prospective customer three things about your brand (and those three things had to be true, provable, and different from what your competitors say), what would they be?
- Look at your positioning statement from BRND-5. What are the distinct claims embedded in it? The unique value, the market category choice, the audience truth: each of these might be a pillar.
- When your best customers refer someone to you, what do they say? Not your words: theirs. What claim are they making on your behalf?
- Is there a claim you make that competitors could also make, but you'd back it up differently? That's still a valid pillar if your proof is stronger.

*Technique — the "say it again" audit:* Ask the founder to look at their last five pieces of content (emails, posts, pitch decks, website sections). What themes keep recurring? The pillars aren't what the founder *wants* to say — they're what the brand already *does* say, formalized and sharpened. If a claim doesn't show up naturally in existing content, it might be aspirational rather than core.*

*Advance when: you have 3–5 candidate pillars, each stated as a clear claim (not a topic or category). "AI-powered" is a topic. "We do in hours what takes an agency weeks" is a claim. Each should connect to the positioning statement.*

**Round 2: Proof Points**
*Extract: 2–3 specific, provable facts or examples that back each pillar — evidence a skeptic would accept.*

For each pillar:

- What's the evidence that this claim is true? Not a testimonial quote: a specific fact, number, case, or demonstration.
- Could a skeptic poke a hole in this claim? If so, what's the strongest counter, and how do you answer it?
- Is this proof current? If the best evidence is from two years ago, is it still representative?

*Technique — the proof hierarchy (Cialdini, §24):* The strongest proof is third-party verifiable (published metrics, independent reviews, industry recognition). Next is customer-verifiable (case studies, testimonials with specific outcomes). Weakest is self-asserted ("we believe," "we're committed to"). Push the founder up the hierarchy: "You say you're the fastest. Can a customer verify that? Is there a metric, a case study, a comparison?"*

*If the founder can't provide proof for a pillar, flag it: "This claim doesn't have provable evidence yet. That doesn't mean it's wrong — it means it's currently an assertion, not a pillar. Want to keep it and build the proof, or swap it for a claim you can already back up?"*

*Advance when: each pillar has 2–3 proof points, at least one of which a skeptic would accept. If any pillar is all self-assertion with no external evidence, it's been flagged.*

**Round 3: Channel Expressions**
*Extract: How each pillar shows up differently across contexts — same claim, different register and entry point.*

For each pillar:

- How does this claim show up on your homepage? What's the headline version?
- How does it show up in a sales conversation or pitch deck? When does it come up, and how is it framed?
- How does it show up in a social post or short-form content? What's the hook?
- How does it show up in a cold email or outreach message? What's the angle that earns attention?

*Technique — the register shift:* The claim stays the same; the register changes. A homepage leads with the outcome ("Look credible without the agency price tag"). A cold email leads with the problem ("Still spending six weeks on brand guidelines?"). A social post leads with the hook ("Your brand deck is 47 slides. Your team reads zero."). Walk the founder through one pillar across all four channels to establish the pattern, then apply it to the rest.*

*Advance when: each pillar has expressions for at least three channels, and the expressions genuinely differ in register and entry point — not just length.*

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Positioning trace test: can each pillar be traced to a specific element of the positioning statement (BRND-5)? If a pillar doesn't connect to the positioning, it's either off-strategy or reveals a gap in the positioning.
- [ ] Substitution test: replace the brand name with a competitor's. Does each pillar still hold? If yes, either the claim isn't differentiated or the proof points need to be sharper.
- [ ] Proof test: does every pillar have at least one proof point that's externally verifiable: not just self-asserted? Pillars without proof are assertions, not messaging.

`AskUserQuestion`
> "Before we write this — [state the single strongest concern as a direct question, e.g., 'Pillar 2 — 'We build lasting brands' — doesn't have any provable evidence yet. It's a strong claim, but without a case study, a metric, or a specific example, it's an assertion your competitors could also make. Do you have proof we haven't surfaced, or should we swap it for a claim you can already back up?']"
- Feels solid: write it
- Let me revisit [element]

## Fill Targets

| Field | Heading | Format |
|---|---|---|
| Pillar heading | `### Pillar N [Headline]` | A short, punchy claim, not a topic or category. Should be ownable by this brand and traceable to the positioning statement. **Layout note:** pillars are parallel blocks, one per pillar. On backends with column layouts (like Notion) each pillar occupies its own column; linear backends stack the pillars sequentially: see the loaded adapter's rendering rules. |
| One-liner | `One-liner:` | One sentence: the claim in its clearest, most portable form. Should work as a standalone sentence anywhere. |
| Proof points | `Proof points:` | 2–3 specific, provable facts or examples. At least one should be externally verifiable. Written as a bulleted list with enough specificity that a skeptic could check them. |
| Channel expressions | `Channel expressions:` | Prose or structured list showing how the pillar shows up across 3–4 channels (homepage, sales deck, social, cold email). Each expression differs in register and entry point, not just length. |

*Pillar headlines: pass the substitution test. If a competitor could use the same headline, sharpen.*
*One-liners: portable — they work ripped from context. Not taglines (those go in BRND-11), but clear, standalone claims.*
*Proof points: at least one per pillar that a skeptic would accept. Self-assertions are flagged, not banned.*
*Channel expressions: genuinely different registers, not the same sentence at different lengths.*
