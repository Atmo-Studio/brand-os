# Voice & Tone Guide: BRND-9
*Voice & Messaging, dimension 2 of 4*

**Fills:** Voice description, Vocabulary table, Tone Modes table, Before/After examples
**Frameworks:** Voice & Tone Spectrum (Flabbi & Content Design London, 2020) → `frameworks.md §19`; Content Design Principles (Podmajersky, 2019) → `frameworks.md §20`; Four Dimensions of Tone (Nielsen Norman Group, 2016) → `frameworks.md §21`

---

## Failure Modes
- Confusing voice with tone: voice is fixed (who the brand is); tone is contextual (how it adapts to the moment). If the "voice" description changes by context, it's actually tone. If the "tone modes" all sound the same, there's no contextual range.
- Writing a voice description that's aspirational instead of diagnostic: the voice should describe how the brand *actually writes now at its best*, not how the founder wishes it sounded. If the voice description doesn't match a single existing piece of content, it's fiction.
- Listing vocabulary without rationale: "we say 'partner' not 'client'" needs a *why*. Without it, the vocabulary table becomes an arbitrary word list that people ignore or forget.
- Skipping the before/after examples: the voice description and vocabulary table are abstract until a writer sees them applied. The before/after bank is where the guide becomes actually usable day-to-day.
- Making tone modes too granular: four modes is the sweet spot. More than five and writers can't remember which mode to use; fewer than three and there's no meaningful contextual range.

---

## Upstream Dependencies

This dimension builds directly on BRND-6 (Brand Personality) and BRND-8 (Brand Story). Voice is personality expressed in language; tone is how that voice adapts across the story's contexts.

- From BRND-6: the personality spectrum, "we are / we are not" guardrails, and personality summary should directly inform the voice description. The voice is the verbal expression of the personality.
- From BRND-8: the brand story's guide character defines the *role* the voice plays: empathetic guide, authoritative expert, supportive peer. The tone modes should map to different moments in the customer's story (problem → plan → success).

*If BRND-6 is incomplete, the voice description will lack grounding. If BRND-8 is incomplete, the tone modes will be arbitrary. Flag either gap: "Voice comes from personality, and tone modes come from the story structure. [Dimension] isn't complete yet — want to go back first?"*

Surface your read: "Across these samples, I see a consistent thread — [describe the voice character]. But it breaks in [context] where it shifts to [different register]. ## Interview Rounds

**Round 1: Voice Character**
*Extract: A prose description of the brand's consistent voice — what any reader would say after encountering the brand's writing across different touchpoints.*

- You described this brand's personality as [personality summary from BRND-6]. If that character had to write to you, sentence by sentence, what would it sound like on the page?
- If someone read five different pieces of your content (a website page, an email, a social post, a support reply, an internal doc) and had to describe how your brand "sounds," what would they say?
- What's the one quality of your writing that should be non-negotiable: the trait that, if it disappeared, would make the writing stop feeling like you?
- When your writing is at its best, what's the effect on the reader? Not what they learn: how they feel. Informed? Energized? Reassured? Challenged?
- Is there a writer, publication, or brand whose writing style is in the neighborhood of where you want to be? Not an exact match: a starting direction.

*Technique, the consistency test:* Voice is what stays the same when tone changes. To isolate it, ask the founder to imagine two very different contexts: a celebration and a crisis. "If your brand had to announce a major win and then handle a service outage in the same day, what stays the same about how you write? That constant is your voice."

*Advance when: you can describe the voice in 3–4 sentences of prose — not a list of adjectives. The description should be specific enough to distinguish this brand from competitors who might use similar adjectives. "Clear and direct" is not enough; "clear and direct, with a warmth that treats the reader as a capable peer rather than a student" is closer.*

**Round 2: Vocabulary**
*Extract: The specific words and phrases the brand uses — and avoids — with reasoning.*

- Are there words your brand uses deliberately: terms you've chosen over their alternatives? "Partners" over "clients," "build" over "create," "people" over "users"?
- What words would feel wrong coming from your brand? Industry jargon you avoid, corporate-speak that doesn't fit, buzzwords you've sworn off?
- Are there words your competitors use that you deliberately don't? Terms that signal a worldview or approach you don't share?
- For each preference: why? What does the word choice signal about the brand's relationship with its audience?

*Technique — the "why behind the word":* Every vocabulary choice encodes a relationship. "Clients" signals a service relationship; "partners" signals collaboration; "people" signals a human-first philosophy. Push the founder to articulate the relationship behind the word, not just the preference. This makes the vocabulary table durable — new writers understand the *principle* and can apply it to word choices the table doesn't cover.*

*Advance when: you have 5–10 word pairs (we say / we avoid) with reasoning. The pairs should reveal a pattern — a consistent philosophy about how the brand relates to its audience through language.*

**Round 3: Tone Modes**
*Extract: 3–4 distinct tone modes the brand uses in different contexts — each defined by when it's used, what it sounds like, and what it doesn't sound like.*

- Let's walk through the different contexts your brand shows up in. When you're celebrating a win (a launch, a milestone, a customer success), how does the writing change? What dials up and what stays the same?
- When a customer is frustrated or something went wrong, how do you handle it in writing? What's the register? Apologetic? Solution-focused? Empathetic but not groveling?
- When you're teaching or onboarding someone (explaining how something works), what does that tone sound like? Patient? Efficient? Conversational?
- Is there a context where urgency matters: deadlines, time-sensitive decisions? How does the writing adapt without losing the brand's character?

*Technique, the "sounds like / doesn't sound like" pairing:* For each tone mode, push for both sides. "Celebratory sounds like 'genuine excitement shared with the reader,' not 'hype, exclamation marks, or self-congratulation.'" The "doesn't sound like" is where the real guidance lives: it prevents the most common misinterpretation of each mode.

*Advance when: you have 3–4 modes, each with a name, a "when" context, a "sounds like" example, and a "doesn't sound like" guardrail. The modes should feel like a range within the same voice — not like different brands.*

**Round 4: Before/After Examples**
*Extract: Real or realistic copy examples showing the voice applied — generic copy rewritten on-brand.*

- Can you share a piece of writing that didn't feel on-brand: something generic, off-tone, or written before the voice was established? Let me rewrite it using what we've defined.
- If you don't have a specific example: let me draft a before/after for a common context: [suggest based on the brand: a homepage hero, a cold email intro, a social media post, a support reply]. You tell me if the "after" feels right.

*Technique, the calibration loop:* Write one before/after pair and show it to the founder. Their reaction calibrates the voice faster than any abstract description. If they say "the after is too [casual/formal/cute/stiff]," that's a refinement signal: adjust and try again. Two or three iterations of this loop produce a sharper voice definition than an hour of abstract discussion.

*Advance when: you have at least 2–3 before/after examples that the founder confirms feel right. These become the calibration tool for anyone writing in the brand's voice — more useful than the prose description alone.*

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Voice vs. tone test: is the voice description truly fixed: would it hold across all tone modes? If the voice description includes contextual variation, it's bleeding into tone. Separate them.
- [ ] Vocabulary coherence test: do the vocabulary choices align with the voice description? If the voice is "warm and conversational" but the vocabulary avoids contractions and uses formal terms, there's a mismatch.
- [ ] Writer usability test: could a new writer (someone who's never met the founder) read this page and write a first draft that wouldn't need a full rewrite? If not, what's missing? Usually it's the before/after examples.

## Fill Targets

| Field | Heading | Format |
|---|---|---|
| Voice description | `## Our Voice` | 3–4 sentences of prose. The brand's consistent voice character: what stays the same across every context. Specific enough to distinguish this brand from competitors. Not a list of adjectives. |
| Vocabulary | `## Vocabulary` table | Table: We say \| We avoid. 5–10 rows minimum. Each pair should encode a relationship or philosophy, not just a preference. Consider adding a "Why" column or inline notes for the reasoning. |
| Tone Modes | `## Tone Modes` table | Table: Mode \| When \| Sounds Like \| Doesn't Sound Like. 3–4 rows: Celebratory, Supportive, Instructional, Urgent (defaults: adapt names to fit the brand). "Sounds Like" is a short phrase or sentence in the brand's voice. "Doesn't Sound Like" is the guardrail: what this mode gets confused with. |
| Before/After examples | `## Before/After Examples` | Repeating toggle blocks. Each toggle: example title, Before (generic or off-brand copy), After (on-brand rewrite). Minimum 2–3 examples covering different tone modes. These should be treated as a living bank: the page improves as more examples are added over time. |

*Voice description: holds true across all tone modes. Describes how the brand sounds, not what it believes.*
*Vocabulary: every pair has a "why" — even if implicit. The pattern across pairs reveals a consistent relationship philosophy.*
*Tone Modes: "sounds like" is a specific phrase, not a restated adjective. "Doesn't sound like" is the guardrail that prevents the most common misinterpretation.*
*Before/After: the most practically useful part of the page. Should be calibrated with the founder — if the "after" doesn't feel right, the voice definition needs refinement.*
