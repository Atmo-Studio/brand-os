## Failure Modes
- Listing competitors without analysis — a table of names and URLs isn't a competitive landscape; it's a bookmark folder
- Choosing axes that don't reveal real strategic differences — if every competitor lands in the same quadrant, the axes are wrong
- Tracking only direct competitors — the real threat is often the status quo (do nothing, use a spreadsheet, hire a freelancer) or an adjacent category entrant
- Writing a strategic read that describes the map instead of interpreting it — "there are four competitors in the top-right quadrant" is description; "the top-right is overcrowded because every entrant leads with the same premium positioning, leaving the accessible-but-expert space unoccupied" is analysis
- Treating the landscape as static — this page needs to be revisited quarterly; a snapshot without a review cadence decays fast

## Interview Rounds

**Round 1 · Identifying the Real Alternatives**
*Extract: The full set of things a customer would do if this brand didn't exist, including non-obvious alternatives.*

- When a potential customer decides not to go with you, what do they do instead? Not just other companies — what's the real alternative? Doing nothing? A spreadsheet? Hiring someone?
- Who do you actually lose deals to? Not who you'd like to compete against — who shows up in the same conversations?
- Is there anyone outside your immediate category who's starting to solve the same problem from a different angle? An adjacent player moving into your territory?
- Are there any brands your customers compare you to that surprise you — comparisons that feel wrong but keep happening?

*Technique — the "do nothing" competitor:* Always include the status quo as a tracked alternative. For many early-stage brands, the biggest competitor isn't another company — it's inertia. Ask: "What percentage of your potential customers just... don't solve this problem at all? What does living with the problem look like for them?"*

*Advance when: you have 3–7 genuine alternatives identified, including the status quo, at least one direct competitor, and any surprising or non-obvious comparisons. If the founder lists more than 7, push for prioritization: "Which 5 of these actually show up when a customer is making a decision?"*

**Round 2 · Choosing the Map Axes**
*Extract: Two strategic dimensions that reveal meaningful differences between competitors — and where this brand's whitespace lives.*

- What are the two things that matter most to your customers when they're choosing between options? Not features — the big trade-offs they're weighing.
- If you had to plot yourself and your competitors on two axes, what would they be? What dimensions actually separate you?
- Where do you think most competitors cluster? Is there a default positioning that everyone gravitates toward — and is that where you want to be?

*Technique — axis validation:* After the founder proposes axes, mentally plot the competitors they named in Round 1. If everyone lands in the same area, the axes don't reveal real differences — push for different dimensions. Good axes produce spread. Test with: "If I plot [competitor A] and [competitor B] on these axes, do they land in meaningfully different places?"

*Advance when: two axes are chosen that produce real differentiation — competitors spread across the map rather than clustering, and at least one quadrant is either empty or sparsely populated.*

**Round 3 · Strategic Read**
*Extract: An interpretation of what the map reveals — overcrowded territory, whitespace, and what it means for this brand's positioning.*

- Looking at where competitors cluster — why do you think they all ended up there? What assumption about the market drives that default positioning?
- Where's the empty space on this map? Is anyone trying to own it? Why or why not?
- Where does your brand sit today — and is that where you want to be? Is there a gap between where you are and where you'd be most valuable?
- If a new competitor entered the market tomorrow with unlimited resources, where would they plant their flag? What does that tell you about what's defensible?

*Advance when: the strategic read names specific overcrowded territory, identifies at least one whitespace opportunity, and connects the whitespace to something this brand can credibly claim. If the founder can't articulate why the whitespace exists, push: "Is that space empty because no one wants it — or because no one's tried?"*

**Round 4 · Competitor Detail (conditional)**
*Run only if: the founder hasn't provided enough detail on individual competitors to fill the Competitors Tracked table — i.e., competitors are named but their axis positions and strategic notes are missing.*

For each competitor (up to the top 5):

- Where does [competitor] sit on the two axes we chose? What's their positioning — in your words, not theirs?
- What do they get right — what's the one thing they do that you respect or that resonates with customers?
- Where do they fall short? What's the gap their customers feel?

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Axis test: do the chosen axes produce genuine spread? If most competitors cluster in one area, the axes may be measuring the wrong dimensions — or measuring dimensions that don't matter to the audience.
- [ ] Completeness test: is the status quo ("do nothing") included as an alternative? Are there any non-obvious or adjacent-category competitors missing?
- [ ] Strategic read test: does the strategic read interpret the map (what it means, what's ownable) or just describe it (who's where)? The read should make a claim about where this brand should plant its flag — not just report what the map shows.
