---

## Failure Modes
- Writing a positioning statement that could belong to any brand in the category. The substitution test is the single most important quality gate on this dimension
- Confusing attributes with value: "we use AI" is an attribute; "you look credible without hiring an agency" is value. The Unique Value table must make this shift explicit
- Choosing a market category that sets the brand up to lose: category framing determines the comparison set; a bad frame means competing on ground where you're weakest
- Treating the positioning statement as a tagline: it's an internal strategic brief, not external copy. It should be precise, not punchy
- Skipping the "do nothing" alternative: the status quo is almost always the most common competitive alternative, and founders almost always forget to include it

## Interview Rounds

**Round 1: Audience Truth**
*Extract: A statement in the customer's language that names who they are, what they're trying to do, and what friction they hit — grounded in the ICP work from BRND-3.*

- Looking at the ICPs we built, when your primary customer tries to [functional job from BRND-3], what actually gets in the way? What's the friction point?
- Why do existing alternatives fail them? Is it that the alternatives are built for a different type of customer, or that they prioritize something your customer doesn't care about?
- If your customer could explain their frustration in one sentence to a friend, what would they say?

*Technique, the Audience Truth template:* The template is `When [audience] tries to [goal], they run into [friction] because existing alternatives are built for [different priority], not [what this audience actually needs].` Don't force the founder's words into this structure prematurely: extract the raw insight first, then shape it together. The template is a compression tool, not an interview crutch.

*Advance when: you can fill the Audience Truth template with specific, ownable language — not generic friction that any competitor could claim to solve.*

**Round 2: Competitive Alternatives & Unique Value**
*Extract: The honest set of alternatives (from BRND-4), what each gets right and wrong, and the specific attributes this brand has that alternatives don't — translated into customer value.*

- We mapped the competitive landscape in BRND-4. Looking at those alternatives, what does each one get right? Be honest.
- Where does each one fall short for your specific customer? Not in general: for the person we described in the ICP.
- What do you have that none of them do? Not a feature list: what capability or approach is genuinely unique to you?
- For each unique attribute: what does it make possible for the customer? Not what it is: what it unlocks. Shift from attribute to outcome.

*Technique, the attribute-to-value ladder:* For each unique attribute, ask "so what?" until you reach a customer outcome. Example: "We use AI" → "So what?" → "We can do in hours what takes an agency weeks" → "So what?" → "You can show up to a board meeting looking credible without spending six weeks and $50K." The last answer is the value.

*Advance when: the Competitive Alternatives table has honest entries for each real alternative (including status quo), and the Unique Value table shows attributes translated to customer outcomes — not features restated as benefits.*

**Round 3: Market Category**
*Extract: How the brand frames its category — the frame that sets the comparison set in the brand's favor.*

- When someone asks "what do you do?", what category do you put yourself in? And does that framing help you or hurt you?
- What category do your competitors claim? Would you be better off in the same category, a subcategory, or an entirely different frame?
- If you frame yourself as a [current category], you get compared to [comparison set]. Is that the comparison you want? What frame would make your strengths the obvious differentiator?

*Technique, the framing test (Dunford, §8):* Category choice is strategic. "Design agency" → compared to every agency. "Brand operating system" → compared to... what? The less crowded the frame, the more the brand owns it, but only if the audience recognizes it. Push: "Would your customer know what a [proposed category] is? If you have to explain the category before explaining the value, the framing might be too novel."

*Advance when: the market category is a frame the audience would recognize, that sets up a comparison set where this brand wins. The template — `This brand is a [category], not a [common misframing]` — should produce a distinction that changes how the audience evaluates the brand.*

**Round 4: Positioning Statement Synthesis**
*Extract: A single positioning statement that synthesizes audience, value, category, and differentiation — using Moore's formula.*

- Don't ask new questions. Draft the positioning statement from the material gathered in Rounds 1–3 and present it for reaction.
- Read the draft aloud (or present it clearly). Ask: "Does this feel true? Does anything feel like a stretch?"
- If the founder pushes back on any element, trace it back to the relevant round and resolve it there.

*The positioning statement formula (Moore, §9):*
`For [specific audience] who want [outcome], [brand] is the [market category] that [unique value]. Unlike [alternatives], [brand] [primary differentiation], so [what becomes possible for the customer].`

*Advance when: the founder confirms the statement feels true, specific, and ownable. Apply the substitution test: replace the brand name with a competitor's — if the statement still holds, the differentiation isn't sharp enough.*

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Substitution test: replace the brand name in the positioning statement with a competitor's. Does it still hold? If yes, the unique value or differentiation isn't specific enough.
- [ ] Category test: does the market category frame set the brand up to win, or to compete on ground where it's weakest? Would a customer recognize the category without explanation?
- [ ] Coherence test: does the positioning statement trace cleanly back to the ICPs (BRND-3) and competitive landscape (BRND-4)? If the audience or alternatives in the statement don't match what was established in those dimensions, something is disconnected.

`AskUserQuestion`
> "Before we lock this in — [state the single strongest concern as a direct question, e.g., 'If I swap your brand name for [competitor X] in this positioning statement, it still reads as true. That tells me the differentiation isn't sharp enough yet. What's the one thing you do that they genuinely cannot?']"
- Feels solid: write it
- Let me revisit [element]
