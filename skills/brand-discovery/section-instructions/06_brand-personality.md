## Failure Modes
- Listing adjectives instead of describing character — "innovative, passionate, authentic" is a word cloud, not a personality; anyone could claim those words. A real personality description makes you feel something specific.
- Treating axes as binary choices — the spectrum is a range, not a toggle. Most brands live somewhere in the middle on most axes, and the interesting insight is *where* exactly and *why*.
- Confusing personality with values — values are what you believe; personality is how you show up. A brand can value rigor and still have a warm, approachable personality.
- Describing who the founder is instead of who the brand is — they're related, but the brand personality needs to survive the founder stepping back from day-to-day communication.
- Skipping the "we are not" — the negative definition is often more useful than the positive one; it tells collaborators where the guardrails are and prevents brand drift.

---

## Upstream Dependencies

This page benefits from BRND-1 (Purpose, Vision, Mission) and BRND-2 (Brand Values). The personality should be *consistent* with the values but is not *derived* from them — a brand that values precision could still have a casual, warm personality.

- From BRND-1: the brand's purpose and vision set emotional context for what kind of character would credibly deliver on them
- From BRND-2: values define behavioral commitments; personality defines how those commitments are expressed

*If BRND-1 or BRND-2 are incomplete, proceed — personality can be explored independently. But flag any contradictions during the gap check.*

## Interview Rounds

**Round 1 · Character Intuition**
*Extract: The founder's gut sense of who this brand is as a character — raw material before the spectrum.*

- If your brand walked into a room, what kind of person would it be? Not a celebrity — a type of person. How do they carry themselves? How do people feel around them?
- Think about the best interaction a customer has ever had with your brand — what made it feel like *you*? What was the quality of that interaction?
- Is there a brand outside your category whose personality you admire — not what they do, but how they show up? What specifically do you admire about their character?
- What's a personality trait you never want your brand to have — something that would feel like a betrayal of who you are?

*Technique — the dinner party test:* If the founder struggles with abstraction, ground it: "Imagine your brand is a person at a dinner party. Are they the one telling stories, asking questions, making quiet observations, cracking jokes? Who gravitates toward them and why?" This produces richer character descriptions than "give me three adjectives."

*Advance when: you have a felt sense of the brand's character — enough to describe it in a sentence or two that feels specific to this brand, not generic.*

**Round 2 · Spectrum Positioning**
*Extract: Where the brand sits on each personality axis — not as a binary choice but as a specific position with reasoning.*

Walk through each axis. For each one, ask the founder to place the brand and explain *why* it sits there. The six default axes are:

1. **Casual ↔ Formal** — "When you write to a customer or show up in public, where do you land? Are you first-name-basis-from-day-one, or is there a professional register you maintain?"
2. **Earnest ↔ Irreverent** — "Do you take your subject matter seriously and expect others to as well? Or do you use humor and irreverence to cut through the noise?"
3. **Grounded ↔ Visionary** — "Do you lead with what's real and proven today — or with where things are going? Practical and here-now, or aspirational and future-facing?"
4. **Quiet confidence ↔ Bold & declarative** — "Do you let the work speak for itself, or do you make strong public claims? Understated or high-volume?"
5. **Approachable ↔ Expert authority** — "Do you want to feel like a peer who happens to know a lot — or a recognized expert people defer to?"
6. **Community-first ↔ Thought leader** — "Do you build community and platform others — or do you lead with your own point of view and expect people to follow?"

*Technique — the "never go past" marker:* For each axis, after placing the brand, ask: "Is there a point on this spectrum where the brand should never go? A line where it stops feeling like you?" This captures the guardrail, not just the position. Some brands have tight ranges; others are comfortable flexing across most of the spectrum depending on context.*

*Advance when: all six axes have a placement with reasoning, and at least 2–3 have a "never go past" marker that reveals where the brand's character breaks down.*

**Round 3 · Personality Summary & Guardrails**
*Extract: A prose description of the brand's character and the "we are / we are not" guardrails.*

- Based on where you landed on the spectrum — if you had to describe this brand's personality in 2–3 sentences to a new copywriter or designer, what would you say? Not a list of traits — a description that gives them a felt sense.
- Give me 3–4 words that describe this brand at its best. The words someone would use after experiencing you at your sharpest.
- Now give me 3–4 words for what the brand is sometimes tempted to be — or what people might mistake you for — but shouldn't be. The "we are not" list.

*Technique — the temptation test:* The "we are not" list is most useful when it names real temptations, not obvious negatives. "We are not boring" tells you nothing. "We are not clever for the sake of clever" tells a writer exactly where the guardrail is. Push: "What's a mode your brand sometimes drifts into that you have to pull it back from?"*

*Advance when: the personality summary is specific enough to guide a creative decision (a designer could read it and know whether a given visual treatment fits), and the "we are / we are not" lists name real qualities and real temptations — not generic positives and obvious negatives.*

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Specificity test: could a competitor in the same category claim this exact personality description? If the summary, the "we are" words, and the spectrum positions could belong to another brand in the space, the personality isn't differentiated enough.
- [ ] Consistency test: does the personality align with the brand's values (BRND-2) and purpose (BRND-1)? A brand whose purpose is rooted in trust and rigor but whose personality is irreverent and bold needs to either reconcile the tension or explain why it's intentional.
- [ ] Actionability test: could a new hire — a copywriter, a designer, a social media manager — read this page and know how to make a judgment call about tone, visual treatment, or language? If not, the description is too abstract.

*Personality Spectrum: positions are described in words with reasoning, not plotted as numbers. The "never go past" markers are the most actionable part.*
*Personality Summary: feels like a person, not a brand deck. A reader should be able to imagine how this brand would handle a specific situation.*
*We are / We are not: the "not" list names real temptations, not strawmen. "Not corporate" is weak. "Not clever at the expense of clarity" is useful.*
