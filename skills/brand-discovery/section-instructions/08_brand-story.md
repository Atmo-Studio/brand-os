## Failure Modes
- Making the brand the hero — this is the single most common and most damaging failure. The customer is the hero. The brand is the guide. If the story is about how great the brand is, it's not a story anyone else wants to tell.
- Writing a 7-part structure that reads like a framework exercise — the table should feel like a real narrative skeleton, not an academic fill-in-the-blank. Each element should connect to the next with narrative logic, not just exist in parallel.
- Conflating the external problem with the internal one — the external problem is what they'd tell their boss; the internal problem is what they'd tell a friend after two drinks. Both need to be distinct and specific.
- Skipping the "failure" element — founders are uncomfortable articulating what's at stake if the customer doesn't act. But without stakes, there's no tension, and without tension, there's no story.
- Writing narrative adaptations (homepage, pitch, social) that are just the 7-part structure copy-pasted into different lengths — each adaptation needs to restructure the story for its context, not just trim it.

## Interview Rounds

**Round 1 · The Character & The Problem**
*Extract: Who the customer is (as a narrative character), what they want, and the three layers of their problem — external, internal, philosophical.*

- We defined your ICP in BRND-3. Let's turn that into a story character. When this person wakes up in the morning, what's the thing they're trying to accomplish? What does "winning" look like for them?
- What's the external problem — the tangible, observable obstacle they'd describe in a work context?
- What's the internal problem — the frustration, self-doubt, or anxiety they wouldn't put in a Slack message but definitely feel? What keeps them up at night about this?
- Is there a philosophical problem — a "this shouldn't be this hard" or "this isn't right" feeling? A sense that the way things work is fundamentally unfair or broken?

*Technique — the three-layer problem:* Miller's framework distinguishes external (tangible), internal (emotional), and philosophical (moral) problems. Founders almost always start with the external problem because it's the easiest to articulate. Push for the internal: "Your customer can describe the external problem in a meeting. But when they're frustrated at 9pm — what are they actually feeling? Is it 'I look incompetent,' 'I'm wasting my time,' 'I shouldn't have to figure this out alone'?"

*The philosophical problem is optional but powerful. It's the "people deserve better than this" claim. Not every brand has one. If it doesn't surface naturally, don't force it — note it as absent and move on.*

*Advance when: you can name a specific character (from the ICP), state what they want, and articulate at least the external and internal problems as distinct layers. The internal problem should feel different from the functional job — more raw, more emotional.*

**Round 2 · The Guide**
*Extract: How the brand shows up as a guide — through empathy (we understand your problem) and authority (we can actually solve it).*

- When this customer first encounters your brand, what do you want them to feel? Not "impressed" — more like "finally, someone who gets it." What's the first signal that you understand their world?
- What gives your brand the right to guide this person? Not just credentials — what experience, perspective, or approach makes you credible?
- How do you show empathy without being condescending? What's the difference between "we understand your pain" and "we've been there too" for your brand?

*Technique — empathy vs. authority balance:* The guide needs both. Too much empathy without authority = "they get me but can't help." Too much authority without empathy = "they're experts but they don't understand my situation." Push the founder to articulate both sides: "If you had to earn trust in two sentences — one that shows you understand their world and one that shows you can actually fix it — what would they be?"

*Advance when: you can describe the guide's empathy (how it demonstrates understanding) and authority (what credibility it brings) as distinct qualities. The empathy should reference the customer's internal problem, not just the external one.*

**Round 3 · The Plan & Call to Action**
*Extract: The clear steps the brand offers (the plan), and the direct and transitional CTAs.*

- When someone decides to work with you — what's the path? Can you describe it in three clear steps? From the customer's perspective, not your internal process.
- What's the one thing you want them to do right now — the direct call to action? "Book a call," "start a trial," "buy now" — what's your version?
- What about someone who's not ready for that yet? Is there a lower-commitment step — a transitional CTA? Something that moves them forward without a full commitment?

*Technique — the three-step plan:* Miller recommends a simple three-step plan that removes ambiguity. The plan should be stated from the customer's perspective ("Tell us what you need → We build it → You launch with confidence") not the brand's internal process. If the founder gives you a 7-step process, push: "Your customer doesn't want to understand your process — they want to know that there IS a process and it's simple. Can we compress this to three steps they'd remember?"

*Advance when: there's a clear three-step plan in customer language, a direct CTA, and ideally a transitional CTA. If the brand doesn't have a transitional CTA, note it as a gap worth filling.*

**Round 4 · Success & Failure**
*Extract: What the customer's life looks like after (success), and what's at stake if they don't act (failure).*

- When your customer succeeds with your help — what does their life look like? Not your deliverables — their world. What's different about their day, their confidence, their reputation?
- What's the transformation? Where did they start, and where do they end up? What can they do now that they couldn't before?
- This one's harder — what happens if they don't act? What's at stake? Not a scare tactic — the honest cost of staying where they are.

*Technique — painting the stakes:* Founders resist the "failure" element because it feels manipulative. Reframe: "This isn't about fear — it's about honesty. If your customer doesn't solve this problem, something real happens. Maybe they stay stuck, maybe they lose credibility, maybe they waste another year. What's the honest cost of inaction?" The failure element shouldn't be a threat; it should be a truthful observation about what's at stake.*

*Advance when: success is described as a transformation (before → after), not a feature list. Failure names a real, honest cost — not a manufactured threat. Both should connect emotionally to the internal problem from Round 1.*

## Gap Check

Run all three internally. Surface the single strongest concern.

- [ ] Hero test: is the customer clearly the hero and the brand clearly the guide? If any element positions the brand as the protagonist ("we built this because we saw a gap in the market"), it needs to be reframed around the customer's journey.
- [ ] Narrative coherence test: do the seven elements connect as a story — character wants something, hits a problem, meets a guide, follows a plan, takes action, and either succeeds or faces consequences? If any element feels disconnected from the narrative arc, trace it back to the source.
- [ ] Internal problem test: is the internal problem distinct from the external one, and does the guide's empathy specifically address it? If the empathy statement only addresses the external problem, the emotional connection is missing.

| 7-Part Structure | `# The 7-Part Structure` table | Table: Element \| This brand's version. Seven rows: The character, The problem (with external/internal/philosophical sub-layers), The guide (empathy + authority), The plan (three steps), Call to action (direct + transitional), Success, Failure. Each cell is 1–3 sentences of specific, ownable narrative — not a generic framework fill. |
| Homepage narrative | `## Homepage narrative structure` | 2–3 paragraphs of prose. | Pitch narrative | `## Pitch narrative` | 2–3 paragraphs of prose. The same story adapted for sales and investor conversations. Usually enters the story at the problem (not the character) because the audience already knows who they are. Should note how the story structure shifts for different pitch contexts. |
| Social & content narrative | `## Social & content narrative` | 2–3 paragraphs of prose. How the story spine manifests in short-form content: what hooks to use, who the character is in each format, where the guide moment lands. Should give a content creator enough structure to write a post without having to re-derive the story from scratch. |

*7-Part Structure: each element connects narratively to the next — not isolated. The problem has all three layers. The guide shows both empathy and authority.*
*Homepage narrative: a copywriter's brief, not a sitemap. Written as flowing narrative, not section labels.*
*Pitch narrative: enters the story differently than the homepage — usually problem-first. Notes the structural shift.*
*Social narrative: specific enough that a social media manager could write a post from it without asking "but what's our story again?"*
