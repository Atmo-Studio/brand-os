# Design principles

**Use when:** Developing or critiquing any composition. Also when work satisfies every principle
and still groups wrong, which is a perception failure rather than an arrangement one, and when a
decision about something a person operates is contested and evidence is wanted rather than taste.

Read [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md) for the intervals these principles resolve
to, [color/color-usage.md](color/color-usage.md) for the contrast floors, and
[typography/typescale.md](typography/typescale.md) for the ratio behind proportion.

The principles are the shared vocabulary of visual design, and learning them is how a designer
stops arguing from preference. Naming the principle at stake turns "this feels off" into a defect
with a cause and a fix. Any of them can be broken by someone who can say why.

---

## The principles of design

How the elements of art behave once someone arranges them. The instruction under each definition
is what an agent runs; the two limits under it are what a critic reads against finished work.

### Alignment

Elements sharing an edge or an axis, whether or not anything sits between them (Williams, 2014).

- **In practice:** put every element on a shared edge or axis, and hold the axis count to what the
  content needs. Alignment is also how a designer produces proximity, which is why it reaches
  further than it looks.
- **Absent:** near-misses read as errors. A viewer cannot tell a small offset from carelessness.
- **Overdone:** everything on a single axis produces a column, not a composition.

### Balance

"The distribution of the visual weight of objects, colors, texture, and space. If the design was a
scale, these elements should be balanced to make a design feel stable" (J. Paul Getty Trust,
2011).

- **In practice:** offset a large quiet element against a small loud one before reaching for
  symmetry. The asymmetrical version carries more energy for the same stability.
- **Absent:** weight collects on one side and the composition reads as unfinished rather than
  dynamic.
- **Overdone:** exact symmetry reads as static. Right for a certificate, wrong for work that has to
  earn attention.

### Contrast

The degree of difference between two elements set in relation to each other, pushed far enough
that a viewer reads the difference as deliberate (Williams, 2014).

- **In practice:** push the difference past the point a viewer could read it as an accident, then
  check the pair against the floor in [color/color-usage.md](color/color-usage.md). Contrast is the
  raw material emphasis spends, and difference spread evenly produces none.
- **Absent:** everything carries equal weight and the eye finds no structure before it finds content.
- **Overdone:** every element competes. Contrast applied everywhere is contrast nowhere.

### Emphasis

"The part of the design that catches the viewer's attention. Usually the artist will make one area
stand out by contrasting it with other areas" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** decide what recedes in the same move that raises the focal point, and hold the
  surface to one. Emphasis is a ratio, so raising an element without lowering the rest produces
  contrast and no focal point.
- **Absent:** the viewer picks their own entry point, and some pick wrong.
- **Overdone:** two focal points are no focal point. The eye alternates and settles nowhere.

### Hierarchy

The ordering of elements by importance, so a viewer knows what to read first without being told
(Lidwell et al., 2010).

- **In practice:** rank the content before styling any of it, and stop at four levels. Scale,
  contrast, position and space are the levers; read
  [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md) for how many one element should carry.
- **Absent:** the surface has content and no argument. The most common defect in otherwise competent
  work.
- **Overdone:** so many ranked levels that the ranking stops being readable.

### Movement

"The path the viewer's eye takes through the work of art, often to focal areas. Such movement can
be directed along lines, edges, shape, and color" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** give required order a path, so the layout carries the sequence without numbering
  anything.
- **Absent:** the viewer reads in whatever order the layout accidentally suggests.
- **Overdone:** so many directional cues that the path loops and the eye never exits.

### Pattern

"The repeating of an object or symbol all over the work of art" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** set the rule once, so a viewer learns it from one instance and applies it to a
  surface nobody showed them. This is what makes an identity reproducible by someone who was not in
  the room.
- **Absent:** every instance has to be read from scratch.
- **Overdone:** repetition with no break becomes texture, and texture carries no information.

### Proportion

"The feeling of unity created when all parts (sizes, amounts, or number) relate well with each
other" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** take the size relationships from one ratio rather than choosing each size, using
  the ratio in [typography/typescale.md](typography/typescale.md). Proportion measures sizes against
  each other; scale measures size against context.
- **Absent:** elements sit in one size band and the piece reads as a list.
- **Overdone:** jumps so large the middle of the range empties, leaving nothing to carry secondary
  information.

### Rhythm

"Created when one or more elements of design are used repeatedly to create a feeling of organized
movement" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** change the interval where the pace should change and hold it where it should not,
  resolving every interval to a multiple of the base unit in
  [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md). Pattern asks what repeats; rhythm asks at what
  interval.
- **Absent:** even intervals throughout, which reads as a table and gets skimmed.
- **Overdone:** intervals changing constantly, which reads as noise rather than pace.

### Unity

"The feeling of harmony between all parts of the work of art, which creates a sense of
completeness" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** name the treatment that repeats across every surface before designing any of
  them. Unity is decided across the set rather than inside any one piece.
- **Absent:** the composition reads as assembled from parts.
- **Overdone:** total unity removes hierarchy, which is the most common way restraint fails.

### Variety

"The use of several elements of design to hold the viewer's attention and to guide the viewer's
eye through and around the work of art" (J. Paul Getty Trust, 2011).

- **In practice:** spend variety on one axis and hold the rest of the system still.
- **Absent:** monotony. A surface with one texture, one weight and one interval stops being read.
- **Overdone:** incoherence, where variety has been spent on so many axes that nothing relates.

### White space

The unoccupied area of a composition, treated as an element rather than as what is left over, and
also called negative space (Lidwell et al., 2010).

- **In practice:** set the space first and fit the content to it, taking the intervals from
  [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md). Space groups elements without drawing a container,
  and sets perceived quality before a word is read.
- **Absent:** density a reader has to work through, which reads as effort demanded rather than
  offered.
- **Overdone:** space with no discernible logic reads as unfinished. The tell is that a viewer cannot
  say what the spacing is doing.

## Gestalt

Everything on a surface takes its meaning from its relation to what surrounds it, and Gestalt is
the account of how (Wertheimer, 1923; Koffka, 1935; Köhler, 1929). Perception organizes a
composition into wholes before anyone consciously reads it, which is why work can satisfy every
principle above and still group wrong.

### Proximity

Objects placed near each other are perceived as belonging together (Wertheimer, 1923).

- **Decides:** whether elements read as one group, before anything is consciously read.
- **Apply it:** move the members closer to each other than to anything around them. Read
  [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md) for the ratio that gap has to clear.
- **Fails when:** a divider or a container cuts through the group. Nothing its members share will
  hold them together after that.

### Similarity

Elements sharing form, color or size are perceived as related, even when separated (Wertheimer,
1923).

- **Decides:** what still reads as related once distance has forced it apart.
- **Apply it:** give the set one treatment in common, strong enough to register at a glance.
- **Fails when:** the treatment is too quiet to register, or so many elements carry it that belonging
  to the set stops meaning anything.

### Common region

Elements inside a shared boundary group more strongly than nearness alone achieves (Palmer, 1992).

- **Decides:** grouping where a layout cannot give a group its own space.
- **Apply it:** draw the boundary only around things that belong together. It groups whatever is
  inside it, intended or not, and beats every grouping decision made within it.
- **Fails when:** a container encloses things that do not belong together.

### Continuity

The eye follows a line or a curve past an interruption (Wertheimer, 1923).

- **Decides:** where the eye goes when a path meets a break.
- **Apply it:** carry a path across the break, or behind an element sitting on top of it.
- **Fails when:** an unintended alignment forms a line the eye follows somewhere it was not meant to
  go.

### Closure

An interrupted shape is completed by the viewer (Wertheimer, 1923).

- **Decides:** how much of a shape can be removed before it stops reading as that shape.
- **Apply it:** imply the form without drawing all of it, which is the basis of most reductive marks.
- **Fails when:** too little of the shape survives the reduction and the mark reads as broken rather
  than as complete.

### Figure and ground

One region reads as the subject and the rest as field (Rubin, 1915).

- **Decides:** what is read as content and what recedes. It resolves before every other perception
  here and corrupts what follows.
- **Apply it:** assign subject and field with contrast and white space together. Check this first
  when a composition is unreadable and nothing else explains it.
- **Fails when:** the assignment is ambiguous and the eye keeps reassigning it.

### Prägnanz

Ambiguous arrangements resolve toward the simplest available reading (Koffka, 1935).

- **Decides:** which reading wins when an arrangement supports more than one.
- **Apply it:** strip competing structure until the reading you intend is the simplest on offer.
- **Fails when:** the simplest available reading turns out to be an unintended shape sitting between
  elements.

**The strongest cue wins and the weaker one goes unseen.** Common region outranks proximity, and
proximity outranks similarity. A group split across a divider does not read as a group whatever
its members share, so adding similarity to repair a proximity failure never works. Move the
elements.

## Laws of UX

Findings from cognitive and behavioral psychology, collected for design by Yablonski (2020), and
the working basis of **human-centered design**: rather than asking a person to adapt to a system,
they describe how people perceive, decide and remember so the system can be built to fit them.
Every line below is a default, and a brief with a stated reason overturns it.

### Fitts's Law

"The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target" (Yablonski,
2020).

- **Decides:** control sizing and placement, and why screen edges and corners carry value.
- **Apply it:** 44 × 44pt minimum on Apple platforms (Apple, n.d.) and 48 × 48dp on Material
  (Google, n.d.), spaced apart, placed where the hand already is.
- **Fails when:** a small target sits far from where the hand rests, or two sit close enough to
  mis-hit.

### Hick's Law

"The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices"
(Yablonski, 2020).

- **Decides:** option count, progressive disclosure, menu depth.
- **Apply it:** cut choices where speed matters, break a complex task into steps, and highlight a
  recommended option.
- **Fails when:** simplifying reaches abstraction and the person can no longer tell what the options
  are.

### Jakob's Law

"Users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know" (Yablonski,
2020).

- **Decides:** where convention is required and where novelty is affordable. It governs controls,
  navigation and forms, and has no claim on what a brand looks like.
- **Apply it:** build on the mental models people already hold, so attention goes to the task rather
  than to learning the interface. Where a change is unavoidable, let them stay on the familiar
  version for a while.
- **Fails when:** novelty lands on the operable half, costing comprehension and buying no
  distinction.

### Miller's Law

"The average person can only keep 7 (plus or minus 2) items in their working memory" (Yablonski,
2020).

- **Decides:** what a person has to carry from one screen to the next.
- **Apply it:** chunk content so it is easier to process, and expect capacity to vary by person and
  by situation.
- **Fails when:** the magical number seven is cited to justify a design limit. It is a finding about
  memory, not about interfaces, and a visible list is not being held in memory.

### Von Restorff effect

"When multiple similar objects are present, the differing one is most likely to be remembered"
(Yablonski, 2020).

- **Decides:** emphasis, with evidence behind it.
- **Apply it:** make important actions visually distinct and use restraint so they do not compete.
  Never carry that distinction on color alone, which excludes people with low vision or a color
  vision deficiency.
- **Fails when:** six things differ, so nothing does.

### Serial position effect

"Users best remember the first and last items in a series" (Yablonski, 2020).

- **Decides:** order in navigation, lists, and any sequence meant to be recalled.
- **Apply it:** put key actions at the far left and right of a navigation, and the least important
  items in the middle.
- **Fails when:** the item that matters most sits in the middle of a long series.

### Aesthetic-usability effect

"Users often perceive aesthetically pleasing design as design that's more usable" (Yablonski,
2020).

- **Decides:** why craft carries functional value, and why polish is dangerous during testing.
- **Apply it:** test what people accomplish rather than what they say about the look.
- **Fails when:** polish buys tolerance for small faults and hides a real one.

### Tesler's Law

"For any system there is a certain amount of complexity which cannot be reduced" (Yablonski,
2020).

- **Decides:** who absorbs the complexity that cannot be removed.
- **Apply it:** take as much of it into the system as the build allows, and decide the remainder
  deliberately rather than by default. Do not design for an idealized rational user.
- **Fails when:** the interface is simplified until the person does the work, and the complexity has
  moved rather than left.

### Doherty Threshold

"Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures neither
has to wait" (Yablonski, 2020).

- **Decides:** perceived performance, and where feedback belongs.
- **Apply it:** give feedback inside 400ms. Where the work genuinely takes longer, spend perceived
  performance on it: progress, animation, anything that makes the wait legible.
- **Fails when:** nothing acknowledges the input, and the person repeats it.

## Choosing which principle leads

Most compositions offer two or more principles that could lead, and only one can. Making that
choice explicitly is the difference between a direction and a collection of correct decisions,
because a surface that satisfies every principle equally reads as nothing in particular. A brief
that states its reason overturns any default below.

1. **Lead with hierarchy over unity.** Where everything agrees and nothing leads, unity is the thing
   failing. Break the system in one place on purpose.
2. **Lead with convention on anything a person operates.** Novelty spent on the operable half costs
   comprehension and buys no distinction.
3. **Hold emphasis to one focal point per view.** A second does not add emphasis, it removes the
   first. A surface carrying two ideas is usually two surfaces.
4. **Lead with alignment over balance.** An unaligned symmetrical layout reads as an accident. An
   aligned asymmetrical one reads as a decision.
5. **Place the group before styling it.** Grouping resolves first in perception, ahead of every
   treatment you could apply to repair it.
6. **Hold variety and unity together rather than trading them.** Unity alone becomes monotony and
   variety alone becomes incoherence. Where the balance sits is what a brand's character decides.

## Refuse

Category defaults, not prohibitions. Terms in `INDEX.md → ## How a discipline is shaped`. Bans are
marked.

- **A rank carried on color alone. A ban.** It excludes people with low vision or a color vision
  deficiency, and no brief overrides that.
- **A pairing below the contrast floor, used as a taste signal. A ban.** Read
  [color/color-usage.md](color/color-usage.md) for the floor and what it covers.
- **A second focal point**, or a focal point raised without lowering anything around it.
- **Exact symmetry reached for as the default balance.**
- **A divider or a container repairing a grouping the spacing failed to make.**
- **More than four ranked levels on one surface.**
- **Repetition with no break**, which stops carrying information and becomes texture.
- **A novel interaction on a routine control.**
- **Variety spent on every axis at once**, or a system holding still on all of them.
- **Space with no discernible logic.** The tell is that a viewer cannot say what it is doing.
- **A directional cue added where the path already reads.**

## Verify

Checks on the finished composition, not on the intention behind it.

- [ ] Exactly one focal point per view, and you can name it.
- [ ] No more than four ranked levels, and the order still reads with the content blurred.
- [ ] No rank carried on color alone.
- [ ] Every group is grouped by its spacing, with no divider doing the work. Read
      [layout/foundations.md](layout/foundations.md) for the ratio.
- [ ] Subject and field are unambiguous at a glance.
- [ ] The size relationships come from one ratio. Read
      [typography/typescale.md](typography/typescale.md).
- [ ] Every pairing that occurs on screen meets the floor in
      [color/color-usage.md](color/color-usage.md).
- [ ] Every operable target meets 44 × 44pt or 48 × 48dp, and feedback lands inside 400ms.
- [ ] One principle is named as leading, and it is written down.
- [ ] Every defect named in review traces to a principle, and the fix addresses its cause.

## Failure conditions

Each principle carries its own limits above. These are the failures of applying the set, and they
survive a composition in which every individual principle checks out.

- **Nothing was chosen to lead.** Every principle is satisfied and the surface reads as nothing in
  particular.
- **The symptom was fixed rather than the cause.** The defect returns on the next surface, because a
  critique that stops at the symptom gives the work nothing it can act on.
- **The principle was retrofitted.** A composition assembled first and argued afterward. The
  retrofit always succeeds, which is why it proves nothing.
- **A finding was cited outside what it found.** A number about memory used as a limit on an
  interface, or a preference about appearance used as evidence of use.
- **A default was treated as a rule.** Every line here is overturnable by a brief that states its
  reason, and refusing to overturn one costs as much as ignoring it.
- **The set was applied surface by surface.** Each piece correct alone, the system incoherent across
  them.
- **An adjective stood in where a value exists.** "Generous," "balanced" and "clean" are
  unverifiable, and the file carrying the number is named above in every case.
